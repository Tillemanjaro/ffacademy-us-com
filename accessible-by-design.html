Take‑home · Not one‑size‑fits‑all

Recipe: start the conversation

Everyone’s barriers are different, so no checklist covers them. These five ingredients open — and keep open — the conversation. Ingredient one starts it.

1 Open the conversation: write an accessibility mission statement ▾

Before tools, before audits, before budget: put a sentence on the table that says who you design for. One or two sentences, public, updated yearly, with leadership buy‑in — like Barclays (“accessible by design… designing for difference means designing for everyone”) or OXO (“it’s not extra steps”). The statement isn’t the finish line; it’s the conversation opener — the thing you point to in every design review, vendor call, and kickoff. Use AI and your brain — start with this prompt:

I work for a [your industry] company and I want to develop an accessibility mission statement. Ask me questions one at a time to make sure you understand my company, our learners, and our values — then help me draft a statement that is concise, invites people to connect, and can be shared publicly.
2 Remember: everyone encounters barriers ▾

Disability isn’t a person’s “problem” to fix — it’s a mismatch between a person and an environment. Fix the environment. Every barrier shows up three ways, and the same design fix serves all three — which means you’re always designing for far more people than “the 1 in 4”:

3 Find YOUR people’s barriers — don’t guess ▾

One‑size‑fits‑all fails precisely because your learners’ barriers aren’t your barriers. Survey your people. Form a small advisory board of learners with disabilities. Before storyboarding, empathy‑map one real learner: what do they say, think, see, hear, feel, and do while taking your course? Ten minutes of empathy mapping catches the “steps you’ve missed” while they’re still cheap to fix.

4 Design multiple ways in: the UDL 3‑question check ▾

Universal Design for Learning, pocket edition — the antidote to one‑size‑fits‑all is multiple means. Ask of every module:

  • Engagement (the WHY): do learners have more than one reason and way to care?
  • Representation (the WHAT): is every key idea available in more than one format — text, audio, visual?
  • Expression (the HOW): can learners show understanding in more than one way?

If a step only works with perfect eyes, ears, hands, and focus — that’s a step you’ve missed.

5 Bring vendors into the conversation ▾

Your LMS and authoring tools are ingredients too. Before you buy (or renew), ask three questions and expect real answers:

  • “How do you test for accessibility — with real assistive‑tech users, or just an automated checker?”
  • “What is your accessibility mission statement?”
  • “What’s on your accessibility roadmap for the next 12 months?”
