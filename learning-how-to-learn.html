Core Science

Principles of Effective Learning

Research-backed principles that instructional designers use to create lasting knowledge transfer. Click any card to learn more.

🧠

Cognitive Load Theory

Our working memory is limited. Effective instruction minimizes extraneous load and channels effort into meaningful processing.

Developed by John Sweller in 1988, CLT identifies three types of cognitive load: intrinsic (complexity of content), extraneous (poor design), and germane (schema formation). Great ID reduces extraneous load through chunking, clear visuals, and sequencing — freeing mental bandwidth for actual learning.
Cognitive Science
🔁

Spaced Repetition

Distributing practice over time dramatically outperforms massed "cramming." The forgetting curve is real — spacing fights it.

Hermann Ebbinghaus mapped the forgetting curve in 1885. Spaced repetition leverages the spacing effect: reviewing material just before you forget it strengthens the memory trace each time. Tools like Anki use algorithms to find optimal intervals. Interval: 1 day → 3 days → 1 week → 1 month.
Memory
🎯

Retrieval Practice

Testing yourself — not just rereading — is the single most powerful study technique. The act of retrieving strengthens memory.

Known as the "testing effect," research by Roediger & Karpicke (2006) showed that a test-study group retained 61% of material vs. 40% for a study-only group a week later. Flashcards, practice problems, and low-stakes quizzes all work. The struggle to retrieve is the learning.
Evidence-Based
🔗

Prior Knowledge Activation

New learning hooks to existing schema. Explicitly activating what learners already know dramatically accelerates understanding.

Ausubel's Assimilation Theory: "The most important single factor influencing learning is what the learner already knows." Advance organizers, analogies, and KWL charts (Know-Want-Learned) all prime the brain's existing networks before introducing new material.
Schema Theory
🌊

Interleaving

Mixing different topics or problem types in a single session feels harder but produces far better long-term retention and transfer.

Interleaving forces the brain to retrieve the right strategy for each problem, building discrimination skills. A study by Rohrer & Taylor (2007) showed interleaved math practice produced 43% accuracy on a final test vs. 20% for blocked practice. It feels inefficient — that difficulty is the point.
Desirable Difficulty
📊

Worked Examples → Fading

Novices learn best from complete examples. As expertise grows, remove scaffolding — the fading effect prevents over-reliance.

The expertise reversal effect: what helps novices (detailed guidance) can actually hurt experts. Instruction should start with fully worked examples, then completion problems (fill in steps), then problem-solving. This mirrors the Gradual Release of Responsibility model: "I do, we do, you do."
Scaffolding
💬

Elaborative Interrogation

Asking "why?" and "how does this connect?" forces deeper processing and creates richer, more transferable memory traces.

When learners generate explanations for facts ("Why would this be true?"), retention improves by 50–60% over simple rereading. The technique activates prior knowledge and forces integration. It's the cognitive mechanism behind the Feynman Technique: explain it simply to understand it deeply.

Instructional Design Models

The structured frameworks professionals use to build learning experiences systematically.

ADDIE Model

The most widely used instructional design framework. A systematic, five-phase process that guides designers from initial analysis through evaluation. Originally developed for the U.S. Army in the 1970s.

  • A
    Analysis — Identify the learning gap, audience, goals, and constraints
  • D
    Design — Map objectives, assessments, content strategy, and media
  • D
    Development — Build and assemble the actual learning materials
  • I
    Implementation — Deliver the training to the target audience
  • E
    Evaluation — Measure effectiveness and iterate

Bloom's Taxonomy

A hierarchical classification of cognitive skills, revised in 2001. Helps designers write precise learning objectives and match assessments to the right cognitive level. Move learners up the pyramid.

  • 1
    Remember — Recall facts (define, list, name, identify)
  • 2
    Understand — Interpret meaning (explain, summarize, classify)
  • 3
    Apply — Use knowledge in new situations (solve, use, execute)
  • 4
    Analyze — Break apart and examine (compare, differentiate, organize)
  • 5
    Evaluate — Make judgments (critique, justify, assess, argue)
  • 6
    Create — Produce new work (design, construct, plan, produce)

Gagné's 9 Events of Instruction

Robert Gagné's framework maps directly to how the brain processes and stores information. Each event corresponds to a cognitive operation. Every effective lesson hits all 9.

  • 1
    Gain learner attention
  • 2
    Inform of objectives
  • 3
    Stimulate prior knowledge
  • 4
    Present the content
  • 5
    Provide learning guidance
  • 6
    Elicit performance (practice)
  • 7
    Provide feedback
  • 8
    Assess performance
  • 9
    Enhance retention & transfer

Cognitive Mapping

Events 1–3
Prepare the brain

Events 4–6
Build the schema

Events 7–9
Cement & transfer

The model mirrors the information processing theory — sensory input → working memory → long-term storage.

Kirkpatrick's Four Levels

The gold standard for evaluating training effectiveness. Donald Kirkpatrick's 1959 model asks: did learners like it, learn it, use it, and did the business benefit? Most organizations only reach Level 1.

  • 1
    Reaction — Did learners find it engaging and relevant? (Smile sheets)
  • 2
    Learning — Did knowledge, skills, or attitudes change? (Pre/post tests)
  • 3
    Behavior — Did they apply it on the job? (Observation, manager reports)
  • 4
    Results — Did the business improve? (KPIs, ROI, outcomes)

SAM (Successive Approximation Model)

Michael Allen's agile alternative to ADDIE. Prioritizes rapid prototyping and iterative feedback over lengthy up-front planning. Ideal for modern, fast-moving organizations.

  • 1
    Preparation Phase — Gather information, define goals
  • 2
    Iterative Design — Rapid prototype → review → refine (repeat)
  • 3
    Iterative Development — Alpha → Beta → Gold builds with stakeholder feedback at each stage

Agile ID Loop

vs. ADDIE:

ADDIE is waterfall — each phase must finish before the next begins.

SAM is agile — prototype early, fail fast, iterate constantly.

Best for: projects where stakeholder feedback is critical and requirements are likely to shift during development.

A Brief History

The Evolution of Learning Science

From Ebbinghaus's memory experiments to AI-powered adaptive learning.

🔬
1885

Ebbinghaus & The Forgetting Curve

Hermann Ebbinghaus memorized and tested himself on nonsense syllables, mapping the exponential decay of memory over time — the foundation of spaced repetition.

🐕
1900s

Behaviorism Takes Over

Pavlov, Watson, and Skinner define learning as conditioned behavior. Drill-and-practice, reinforcement schedules, and programmed instruction dominate classrooms.

🧠
1956

Bloom's Taxonomy Published

Benjamin Bloom and colleagues publish the Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, giving educators a shared language for learning goals across six cognitive levels.

💻
1965

Gagné's Conditions of Learning

Robert Gagné publishes a systematic framework tying instruction to cognitive processes — the 9 Events of Instruction become a cornerstone of ID practice.

🏛️
1975

ADDIE Formalized

Florida State University develops ADDIE for the U.S. Army. Its systematic approach to training design spreads across military, corporate, and academic contexts.

📱
2000s

eLearning Explosion

SCORM standards, Learning Management Systems, and Flash-based courseware bring instructional design into the digital age. Accessibility and scalability transform training.

🤖
Today

Adaptive Learning & AI

AI-powered systems personalize learning paths in real-time. Microlearning, xAPI, and data analytics enable designers to close skill gaps with unprecedented precision.
