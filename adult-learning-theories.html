🧠 Module 01 · Adult Learning Theory

Andragogy: The Art &
Science of Teaching Adults

Malcolm Knowles coined the term andragogy in 1968 to distinguish adult learning from pedagogy — the teaching of children. His model rests on six core assumptions about what makes adults tick in the learning environment.

Theorist Profile

Malcolm Shepherd Knowles

1913–1997 · American educator and author. Knowles introduced the concept of andragogy to the United States, publishing The Modern Practice of Adult Education in 1970. His work fundamentally reshaped how organizations design training programs.

"The richest resource for learning in any situation is the learner themselves — their experience, curiosity, and motivation to grow."
— Malcolm Knowles, paraphrased from The Adult Learner (1973)

The 6 Principles of Andragogy

Click each principle to explore what it means and how to apply it.

1
Self-Concept — The Need to Know
⌄
Adults need to understand why they are learning something before they commit to learning it. Unlike children, they don't accept learning simply because an authority figure says so. As learners mature, their self-concept moves from dependency to increasing self-directedness.

In practice: Always start with a clear "why" — connect content to real-world outcomes learners care about.
2
Learner Experience — The Foundation to Build On
⌄
Adults bring a wealth of experience to every learning situation. This experience is not just background — it is a rich resource for learning. Andragogy emphasises experiential techniques like discussion, simulation, and problem-solving over passive reception.

In practice: Use case studies drawn from learner contexts. Facilitate peer-sharing activities that leverage accumulated expertise.
3
Readiness to Learn — Life Roles Drive Motivation
⌄
Adults are ready to learn when content is relevant to their current life or work roles. They are particularly receptive to learning that helps them cope with transitions — a promotion, a new technology, a change in family structure.

In practice: Time learning interventions to coincide with role transitions and real-world needs.
4
Orientation to Learning — Problems, Not Subjects
⌄
Children are content-oriented; adults are problem-oriented and performance-centred. Adults want to apply new knowledge immediately. They learn best when content is organised around real problems rather than academic subjects.

In practice: Use problem-based learning (PBL) scenarios. Frame every lesson around a challenge the learner will actually face.
5
Motivation to Learn — Intrinsic Over Extrinsic
⌄
While adults respond to external motivators (salary increases, promotions), the most potent motivators are internal: self-esteem, quality of life, job satisfaction, curiosity. Mandated training that ignores intrinsic motivation typically yields low engagement.

In practice: Offer choice in how learners demonstrate mastery. Tie learning to personal values, not just organisational metrics.
6
Self-Direction — Autonomy in the Learning Process
⌄
As people mature, their psychological need to be self-directing increases. Adults resent being treated as passive recipients of information. They want ownership of their learning journey — setting goals, selecting methods, and evaluating outcomes.

In practice: Provide learning paths with options, not rigid linear sequences. Support learner agency at every stage.

Transformative Learning:
When Minds Shift

Jack Mezirow's Transformative Learning Theory (1978) describes one of the most profound changes possible in a learner: a fundamental shift in how they see themselves and the world. It's learning that changes not just what you know, but who you are.

1978
Year Mezirow first published his theory
10
Steps in the transformative process
∞
Potential for ongoing perspective growth
"Transformative learning involves experiencing a deep, structural shift in the basic premises of thought, feelings, and actions."
— Jack Mezirow, 2000

Core Concepts — Tap to Explore

🏗️
Meaning Structures
Tap to reveal

The assumptions, beliefs, and expectations we use to interpret experience. They act as invisible filters through which we see every situation. Transformative learning challenges these structures.

💥
Disorienting Dilemma
Tap to reveal

A crisis or challenge that disrupts your current frame of reference. It could be a job loss, health scare, or simply encountering a perspective that profoundly contradicts your own. This is the trigger point for transformation.

🔭
Perspective Transformation
Tap to reveal

The end goal — a fundamental change in how you see yourself and the world. Mezirow calls this a shift in frames of reference. It's lasting, identity-level change, not just new knowledge.

🧩
Frames of Reference
Tap to reveal

The collection of assumptions and expectations that filter our experience. They include habits of mind (broad orientations) and points of view (specific attitudes). Both can be transformed.

💬
Critical Reflection
Tap to reveal

The process of questioning our assumptions and their sources. Mezirow argues this is the distinguishing feature of adult learning — the ability to critically examine why we believe what we believe.

🤝
Discourse
Tap to reveal

Dialogue with others who can challenge and validate our transformed perspective. Transformation rarely happens in isolation — it requires social engagement and testing new views with trusted others.

Mezirow's 10-Step Transformative Process

Transformation isn't instant. Mezirow mapped a journey that learners move through, though not always linearly.

Step 1
A Disorienting Dilemma
An experience disrupts your existing frame of reference. A crisis, a profound challenge, a perspective you cannot reconcile with your own.
Step 2
Self-Examination
Feelings of guilt, shame, fear, or discomfort prompt deep personal examination. This is uncomfortable but necessary.
Step 3–4
Critical Assessment of Assumptions
The learner begins questioning their previously held beliefs. They recognise that their assumptions may be distorted, incomplete, or unfair.
Step 5–6
Recognising Shared Experience & Exploring Options
The learner realises others have shared this transformation. New roles, relationships, or actions become imaginable — the horizon broadens.
Step 7–8
Planning & Acquiring Knowledge
A plan of action emerges. The learner actively seeks knowledge and skills needed to act on their new perspective.
Step 9–10
Trying New Roles & Reintegration
The learner tests new behaviours, builds competence, and ultimately reintegrates into their life with a transformed perspective — a new way of being.
🎯 Real-World Scenario

James is a 52-year-old factory supervisor with 20 years of experience. After participating in an immersive inclusion workshop, he finds himself deeply uncomfortable — he realises he's held implicit biases he was never aware of. He's questioning assumptions he's held his whole life.

Where is James in Mezirow's transformative process?

⚠️
Important Note: Transformative learning is not always comfortable. Designers must create psychologically safe spaces for critical reflection — learners need to feel safe enough to challenge their own deeply held assumptions without fear of judgment.

Designing for Transformation

🪞

Build in Reflection

Structured reflection activities — journaling, peer dialogue, debriefs — give learners space to process disorienting experiences rather than simply moving on.

🌊

Create Disorienting Moments

Use case studies, simulations, and counter-narratives that deliberately challenge assumptions. Comfort-zone learning rarely transforms.

🤝

Foster Discourse

Build in collaborative dialogue. Small-group discussion, Socratic questioning, and peer coaching all accelerate perspective transformation.

🔐

Ensure Psychological Safety

Transformation requires vulnerability. Explicitly establish ground rules, model openness yourself, and protect learners from judgment during sensitive reflection.

Self-Directed Learning:
Owning the Journey

Self-Directed Learning (SDL) describes a process in which individuals take the initiative — with or without the help of others — in diagnosing their learning needs, setting goals, identifying resources, and evaluating outcomes. It is perhaps the most adult of all adult learning characteristics.

Foundational Theorist

Malcolm Knowles

Positioned SDL as a core characteristic of adult learners. Defined it as a process where learners take initiative in planning, carrying out, and evaluating their own learning experiences.

Extending the Theory

Allen Tough (1971)

Found that 70% of adults' learning projects are self-directed. His research revealed that adults conduct an average of 8 self-directed learning projects per year — each lasting ~100 hours.

"In its broadest meaning, self-directed learning describes a process by which individuals take the initiative — with or without the assistance of others — in diagnosing their learning needs, formulating goals, identifying resources, and evaluating learning outcomes."
— Malcolm Knowles, Self-Directed Learning (1975)

The SDL Readiness Spectrum

Learners aren't uniformly self-directed. Hiemstra & Brockett's PRO (Personal Responsibility Orientation) model reminds us that readiness varies by individual, context, and topic.

🟡 Dependent Learner 🟢 Self-Directed Learner
🟡
Dependent
Needs direction, clear structure, and frequent feedback. Content-driven approach works best.
🔵
Interested
Motivated but lacks skills. Requires coaching, scaffolded tasks, and guided reflection to grow independence.
🟢
Self-Directed
Intrinsically motivated and capable. Thrives with open-ended projects, autonomy, and minimal oversight.

The SDL Process

🔍

1. Diagnose Learning Needs

The self-directed learner starts by honestly assessing gaps between their current capabilities and desired outcomes — without waiting for a trainer to tell them what they need.

🎯

2. Formulate Learning Goals

Goals are specific, personally meaningful, and tied to real-world performance. The learner owns them — they're not handed down from a curriculum or manager.

📚

3. Identify Human & Material Resources

Experts, peers, books, courses, communities, mentors — the SDL learner actively curates their own learning ecosystem rather than waiting for a course to be assigned.

🚀

4. Implement Learning Strategies

The learner chooses methods that suit their style, context, and goals — reading, doing, experimenting, observing, discussing. No single method imposed from outside.

📊

5. Evaluate Learning Outcomes

Self-assessment is built in. The learner reflects on what they've achieved, what gaps remain, and what they'd do differently — closing the loop and beginning again.

♾️
A Continuous Cycle
Self-directed learners don't stop at one cycle — each completed project reveals new gaps and ignites new goals.
